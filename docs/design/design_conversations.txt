A design philosophy that stays consistent with the rest of the project (pathways as the organizing spine, blueprints as pure functions of local context + seed, serfs as the agents that realize the plan, reserved margins for construction) can be built from a few interlocking principles.

Pathways define the public edge.

Every building claims a frontage along a designated pathway. Distance from the path edge varies within a small range (the existing 0.25–1 m band, or a slightly wider one for larger structures). The front wall is the primary public face. A short private path or apron connects the exterior door to the public pathway. Curving pathways are handled by sampling the path direction and local tangent at the chosen frontage point; the building orients to that local frame rather than to a global axis. Shared walls occur only between adjacent frontages on the same path segment, at a controlled rate, and only when the two buildings agree on height and construction type for the shared face.

Massing is hierarchical and expandable.

A building starts as a base volume (footprint + wall height + roof type) derived from the pathway frontage, local terrain, zone rules, and a style seed. Expansion is explicit: “out” adds a side or rear volume that must still respect the construction margin and neighbor clearance; “up” adds a storey that inherits the lower frame and may introduce an exterior stair or overhanging walkway. The style seed and the building’s function (hovel, bakery, forge, etc.) bias which expansions are legal. A forge prefers an open ground floor with lodging above; a bakery prefers a public ground-floor entrance with housing above; adjacent workshops may share a single exterior stair and a connecting walkway at the upper level. These are constraints on the massing stage, not after-the-fact decorations.

Terrain is resolved before detailing.

Uneven ground is handled at the massing stage by a combination of pad levelling, short retaining walls, and limited terraforming (cut/fill within a budget the serfs can perform). The reserved construction margin already includes space for scaffolding; it can also include space for a retaining wall or a short flight of steps. Once the pad and any retaining structure are accepted, the building frame is generated relative to that resolved ground. Roofs, overhangs, and upper walkways are derived from the frame edges rather than from the raw terrain.

Openings and secondary elements are frame-relative.

Doors, windows, and exterior stairs are punched into or attached to the structural frame after massing is locked. Door placement prefers the pathway side; rear doors are allowed on a lower probability and only when the rear has clearance. Windows are constrained to bays between posts. An exterior stair or overhanging walkway is a first-class element that claims space in the margin and connects specific levels. Because these are derived from an already-accepted frame, they cannot collide with the structure or with neighbors.

Style and function supply the palette, not the geometry.

Construction type (timber frame with thatch, stone with wooden roof, etc.) is a parameter that selects materials, roof pitch ranges, opening styles, and which expansions are preferred. It does not freely randomize primary geometry. Decorative filling of residual space (courtyard objects, alley fences, woodpiles, small props) happens last, inside areas that the massing stage has already marked as non-structural. These props are cheap, seed-driven, and do not affect navigation or construction stages.

Uniformity comes from the shared constraints; uniqueness comes from the seed and the local context.

Two buildings on the same pathway with the same style seed and similar terrain will share wall heights, roof language, and opening rhythm. Different seeds or different functions produce different expansions, different material accents, and different secondary arrangements, but both still read as the same settlement because they obey the same frontage, margin, and frame rules. The result is a settlement that feels planned along its paths without looking stamped from a single template, and that can grow (up or out) without requiring a global replan.

This keeps the generator layered the same way the rest of the engine is layered: constrain the space and the structural frame first, derive everything else from that frame, and let the serfs execute the resulting plan as a sequence of edits. The details of how any particular stage is computed can be filled in later; the philosophy is that no stage is allowed to violate the constraints established by the stages before it.

Wages and household spending

Your guard example illustrates this particularly well.
- The lord collects taxes and levies.
- The treasury pays the guards.
- Guards purchase food, clothing, equipment and services.
- Merchants receive income.
- Merchants purchase materials and employ workers.
- Workers spend their wages.
- The resulting transactions generate further demand and taxable economic activity.
The guards aren't simply an expense. They're also participants in the economy.

Pay them too little and their purchasing power, retention and effectiveness suffer.

Pay them too much and the treasury loses capacity to fund other responsibilities. Depending on how the money supply and production respond, excessive spending can contribute to inflation.

This creates an important distinction between wealth, income, liquidity and productive capacity.

A wealthy settlement isn't necessarily a settlement with enough available goods to satisfy demand.
3. The economy creates its own constraints
I particularly like your potter example.

The potter needs clay.

The clay producer needs tools.

The toolmaker needs metal.

The miner needs food and equipment.

The farmer needs labour.

The labourers need wages.

Suddenly, the availability of clay isn't just a geological or resource-generation question. It's an economic question.

The clay might exist in abundance, but if the potter cannot afford it, production stops.

Likewise, a merchant might have plenty of money but be unable to purchase something that nobody produces.

This is where your simulation can generate problems without scripted events.
4. The transition from free labour to wages
I think this deserves particular attention because it fundamentally changes the nature of the settlement.
Early settlement	Developed society
Communal labour	Employment
Shared basic provisions	Household purchasing
Simple resource allocation	Market transactions
Basic production	Specialized industries
Collective storage	Commercial inventories
Direct obligations	Wages, taxes and levies

5. The most important architectural principle
I would be very careful about one thing as this develops.

Don't confuse money circulation with wealth creation.

Money can circulate rapidly while the settlement produces very little.

Likewise, production can increase substantially without everyone having enough purchasing power to participate in the economy.

I'd keep distinct concepts for:
- Physical inventories.
- Productive capacity.
- Household wealth.
- Business capital.
- Wages and income.
- Treasury funds.
- Outstanding obligations.
- Prices and purchasing power.
These distinctions will become increasingly important as the economy grows.

For example, taxation transfers purchasing power to the treasury; it doesn't automatically destroy the underlying wealth. Government spending then redistributes that purchasing power, while the actual goods and labour consumed by government projects still have opportunity costs.

That distinction will help prevent economic systems from becoming a collection of arbitrary resource counters.

The first is a production and distribution problem.

One exterminates the wolves, restricts deer hunting, and eventually faces a poaching crisis. Another tolerates wolves, develops livestock protection, and establishes regulated hunting. Their economies, industries, food reserves, and eventual settlements diverge.

Neither needed a different scripted campaign. They created different histories through their decisions.

That is the distinction between procedural content and emergent history. Procedural generation gives you different starting conditions. Emergent simulation gives those conditions meaning through everything that happens afterward.

And I think this is the most important point in your entire concept:

You don't want the player to build a medieval settlement. You want the player to govern a society that builds its own settlement.

The player should eventually look at their town and recognize that its layout, industries, institutions, prosperity, and problems are the accumulated consequences of decisions made throughout the game.

Not a blueprint they executed. A history they created.

That's a much stronger foundation for replayability than simply generating another map or rearranging building models.

I think the strongest aspect of this design is that its complexity is largely compositional rather than bespoke.

You're not proposing hundreds of individually engineered behaviours. You're proposing a relatively small set of consistent rules that interact:

    Needs and wants establish individual motivations.

    Skills and aptitude determine capability.

    Positions and vacancies create opportunities.

    Demand and scarcity influence production.

    Risk and reward influence behaviour.

    Resources and infrastructure constrain what is possible.

    Social relationships and authority influence cooperation and conflict.

Once those foundations are balanced, new industries, occupations, ecological relationships, and economic dependencies can emerge without each needing its own elaborate decision-making system.

That is where resisting overengineering matters. If every occupation needs a custom AI, every shortage needs a scripted event, and every social problem needs a special-case solution, you lose the very coherence you're trying to create.

Your scripted content then has a different purpose. Traveling merchants, vagabonds, entertainers, foreign relations, diplomatic demands, and battles provide external stimuli and memorable encounters. They introduce circumstances into a world that already has the mechanisms to react to them.

And the player experience becomes fundamentally different from conventional colony management.

You aren't pausing every few seconds to tell six people what to do. You're deciding whether the settlement can afford another industry, whether winter reserves are sufficient, whether the roads need protection, whether a promotion is sustainable, or whether a neighbouring lord's demands should be accepted.

The inhabitants handle their lives. The player handles the responsibilities of leadership.

One technical caution worth preserving: simple behavioural rules still need observable outcomes, consistent accounting, and instrumentation. A small number of parameters can produce enormous complexity, but their interactions need to remain understandable and balanceable.

The ambition isn't to simulate everything individually. It's to establish a world where a relatively small number of rules make everything matter.

That's a sound foundation for the game you're describing.
